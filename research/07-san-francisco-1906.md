# 1906 San Francisco Earthquake and Fire

**Scenario ID:** 07-san-francisco-1906  
**Dates:** 18–21 April 1906  
**Location:** San Francisco, California, United States  
**Production title:** *When the Ground Stopped, the Fire Continued*

## Executive summary

At 5:12 a.m. on 18 April 1906, a magnitude 7.9 earthquake ruptured the San Andreas Fault near San Francisco. Shaking lasted less than a minute, but broken water mains left firefighters unable to contain fires that burned for three days. Contemporary newspapers recorded fear, damaged communications, and provisional casualty estimates. Later government and scientific accounts place deaths at roughly 3,000 and about half of the city’s 400,000 residents homeless. A responsible interactive world should foreground mutual aid, infrastructure failure, and unequal relief rather than spectacle.[1] [2] [5] [7]

## Full article

Before sunrise on Wednesday, 18 April 1906, many San Franciscans were still at home, in hotels, or in lodging houses when the ground first stirred. At almost exactly 5:12 a.m., a foreshock was felt around the Bay Area. Twenty to twenty-five seconds later, the main shock arrived. Strong shaking lasted roughly 45 to 60 seconds, long enough to damage buildings, crack streets, and break water infrastructure on which the city would soon depend.[2]

The event was regional, not simply a downtown calamity. Modern United States Geological Survey analysis identifies a magnitude 7.9 earthquake and a rupture along the northernmost 296 miles of the San Andreas Fault, from northwest of San Juan Bautista to the Cape Mendocino triple junction.[1] [2] This is later scientific reconstruction, based on geological and seismological evidence. People on 18 April did not have that framework. They observed falling chimneys and walls, interrupted transport and communications, and fires beginning soon after the shaking.

The fires made a short earthquake into a three-day urban emergency. The National Archives says they destroyed nearly 500 city blocks; the National Park Service describes about thirty fires breaking out almost immediately and more than 500 blocks destroyed. Both accounts agree on the central constraint: fractured water pipes made organized firefighting drastically harder.[5] [6] Residents moved toward parks, the Presidio, ferry routes, and other perceived places of safety, carrying blankets and what supplies they could manage. Emergency aid later included tents, food, water, blankets, and medical supplies, while many survivors lived for weeks in temporary encampments.[5]

Two newspapers show how reporting itself became part of the disaster. The *San Francisco Call* of 19 April appeared as a joint issue with the *Chronicle* and *Examiner* after the papers’ normal operations were disrupted.[3] Its front-page article, “Earthquake and Fire: San Francisco in Ruins,” records people moving with blankets toward Golden Gate Park and describes broken water supply, dynamiting, injuries, and a city still being consumed by fire. Its language is urgent and theatrical, calling the city a “mass of smouldering ruins”; it also admits that the number dead was not known, although it gave an estimate of at least 500.[3]

The *New York Times*, publishing the same day from a San Francisco dateline, similarly led with 500 dead, $200 million in losses, and 50,000 homeless. Its account places particular emphasis on failed water mains, broken telegraph and telephone links, the destruction of large buildings, and troops guarding streets.[4] Read together, the papers agree on immediate physical failures and mass flight, but their perspectives differ. The *Call* is a damaged local institution reporting from inside a burning city; the *Times* packages a fast-moving local dispatch for a national audience through headline-scale totals, infrastructure failure, and security. Neither early total is a final death count, and their reporting of military orders should be attributed to the papers rather than reenacted as settled legal fact.

Later public accounts revise the scale. The National Archives estimates about 3,000 deaths and says half of a city of 400,000 was left homeless. USGS likewise cautions that the often-quoted 700 deaths is an underestimate by a factor of three or four.[2] [5] These are retrospective estimates, not numbers available to people during the first day. They help correct a historical record in which the human cost was long understated.

Relief was not experienced equally. The National Park Service reports that an estimated 15,000 Chinese residents of Chinatown lost nearly everything and that Chinese refugees were the only ethnic group segregated in refugee camps. It further documents efforts by some officials to use the disaster to move Chinese San Franciscans away from Chinatown before the plan failed and rebuilding proceeded.[7] This history belongs in any reconstruction of the city’s recovery. It should be presented with named sources, clear labels for interpretive elements, and no suggestion that displacement or exclusion was an inevitable side effect of the earthquake.

For a 3D learning world, the central story is not a thrill of collapse. A regional fault rupture damaged an unequal city; a fire emergency followed; people sought shelter, information, food, and family; and institutions made consequential choices about aid and belonging. Primary images and film need careful reading: the Library of Congress notes that some intertitles in its 1906 aftermath film are sensationalized and that one family meal scene may have been staged.[8]

## Reactor world-generation prompt

> **Create a respectful, evidence-led 3D learning environment titled “After the Shock: San Francisco, 18–21 April 1906.”** The active scene is an explicitly **interpretive reconstruction** of a documented Golden Gate Park relief camp on **Thursday, 19 April 1906, 8:30–10:30 a.m., Pacific local time**. Locate it in the real geography of **Golden Gate Park, San Francisco, California**; do not invent a precise historical tent coordinate, since the National Archives caption documents a Golden Gate Park camp but does not establish an exact placement. A non-playable orientation map shows the relation of the park to the Presidio, Market Street, Chinatown, the Ferry Building, and the San Andreas Fault. Use a second, non-traversable archival-map overlay of the devastated central city rather than placing the learner in an active fire zone.
>
> Use a human-scale first-person and optional slow third-person camera: eye height 1.65 m, walking pace only, no running, jumping, collision comedy, or aerial drone fly-through. Make paths wide and accessible; model tents, temporary cooking and supply areas, water containers, bread crates, blankets, handcarts, horse-drawn wagons, posted relief notices, and a small medical-aid station. The built environment should show 1906 canvas tents, park paths, trees, and distant smoke haze, with no modern pavement markings, cars, skyscrapers, plastic, electrical signage, or contemporary emergency equipment. Light the scene with diffuse early-April morning sunlight filtered by distant smoke; label weather and the exact camp layout as interpretive where not directly documented. Keep fire visually distant and subdued: gray-brown haze and a far skyline, no cinematic flames.
>
> Populate the scene with a calm, mid-density crowd of about 40–60 people: displaced residents, relief workers, and U.S. Army personnel, with period-appropriate wool suits, waistcoats, shirts, long skirts, practical jackets, hats, shawls, boots, and olive-drab U.S. Army service clothing. Props and clothing must show ordinary 1906 material culture without caricature. Do not tokenise ethnicity. Include a clearly marked learning panel that explains, with attribution, that Chinese San Franciscans were segregated in refugee camps and that officials attempted to displace Chinatown; do **not** place Chinese residents in an unexamined generic integrated camp or invent individual biographies.
>
> Create six quiet interactive hotspots: (1) a fault-and-ground map explaining the later USGS reconstruction of the 296-mile rupture and magnitude 7.9; (2) a broken-water-main model showing why fire response failed; (3) a source desk comparing the 19 April *San Francisco Call* and *New York Times* headlines and their provisional casualty figures; (4) a relief ledger that traces tents, food, water, blankets, medical supplies, sanitation, and congressional appropriations; (5) a Chinatown context map about segregation, relocation pressure, and rebuilding, sourced to NPS; and (6) an archival-image viewer that pairs the National Archives Golden Gate Park camp photo with a “what this image can and cannot prove” note. Every panel distinguishes **contemporary report**, **archival record**, **later scientific analysis**, and **interpretive reconstruction**.
>
> Narration should be calm, precise, and compassionate, in the voice of a public historian: name uncertainty, avoid melodrama, and center safety, mutual aid, infrastructure, and unequal access to relief. No invented dialogue, no claims of personal memory, and no undated “facts.” Include an always-available content note and a leave-scene option. Explicitly exclude earthquake shaking simulation, collapsing-building gameplay, bodies, injuries, death sounds, screams, fire-as-entertainment, looting or shooting mechanics, weapons interactions, chase/score systems, reenacted executions, disaster tourism, racial slurs, “exotic” Chinatown imagery, and any implication that segregated relief was natural or inevitable. The learner never earns points for surviving, controlling crowds, or extracting resources.

## Interaction design

1. **Layered evidence map:** Toggle “contemporary account,” “later science,” and “interpretive reconstruction” to see the rupture, broken water system, relief locations, and city geography without mistaking one evidence type for another.
2. **Water and fire systems table:** Manipulate a simplified, non-game model of ruptured mains and limited hose pressure. The payoff is an explanation of infrastructure interdependence, not a challenge to save buildings.
3. **Newspaper comparison desk:** Read short, transcribed excerpts and headlines from the *San Francisco Call* and *New York Times* alongside later death/displacement estimates. Learners identify what reporters knew on 19 April and what later evidence revised.
4. **Relief logistics ledger:** Explore how tents, food, water, blankets, medical care, and sanitation moved through camps. Include staff notes on uncertainty and the limits of institutional response; no queue-management scoring.
5. **Chinatown policy lens:** A source-based map and timeline explains camp segregation, attempted relocation, and rebuilding. It requires reflection on civic power and discrimination rather than role-playing displacement.
6. **Archival-image literacy station:** Compare a National Archives camp photograph with the Library of Congress’s caution about sensationalized film intertitles and possible staging. Learners annotate “documented,” “inferred,” and “unknown.”

## Core caveat

The most important constraint is that the disaster’s death toll, individual experiences, and the exact layout of relief sites remain partly uncertain, while the unequal treatment of Chinese San Franciscans is historically documented. The world must never convert uncertainty into invented personal drama or convert trauma into a survival game; every reconstruction needs provenance labels and a path to the underlying sources.
