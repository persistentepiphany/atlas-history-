## 70–100 word summary

In April 1955, representatives of 29 Asian and African governments met in Bandung’s Merdeka Building, newly prepared as an international venue. Sponsored by Burma, Ceylon, India, Indonesia, and Pakistan, the gathering addressed cooperation, self-determination, racial discrimination, colonial rule, economic development, and the risk of wider war. Its 24 April Final Communiqué set out ten principles for peaceful relations and practical ideas for economic and cultural cooperation. Bandung did not erase differences among participants, but it demonstrated that recently independent states could meet on their own terms and shape international debate. [1] [5]

## Full article: A hall remade for a different conversation

On the morning of 18 April 1955, delegates and guests entered Bandung’s newly reconstructed Merdeka Building: the *New York Times* reported an audience of nearly 2,000 and noted the varied dress of people from many countries. The setting carried its own history. The building had begun as Societeit Concordia, a social venue associated with Europeans in colonial Bandung; Indonesia renamed it Merdeka (“freedom”) on 7 April, shortly before the conference. For a week, its hall became a place where representatives from Asian and African governments could address one another without a European imperial power convening the room. [2] [7]

The lead-up was deliberate. Burma, Ceylon, India, Indonesia, and Pakistan invited the meeting, and the final communiqué records 24 additional participating countries. Preparatory discussions at Bogor in late 1954 had favored a goodwill conference on shared questions rather than binding participants to every dispute. The stakes were nevertheless high: much of Asia and Africa was still under colonial rule, racial segregation structured public life in parts of Africa, and the Cold War raised fears of a wider and potentially nuclear war. A U.S. State Department memorandum from 7 April shows Washington officials also wondering whether Bandung might affect tensions in the Taiwan Strait. [1] [2] [6]

The conference’s record is broader than a single speech or photograph. It examined economic and cultural cooperation, human rights and self-determination, dependent peoples, and peace. The communiqué called for technical exchanges, research and training, trade cooperation, support for the United Nations’ human-rights principles, and an end to racial segregation and discrimination. It declared colonialism “in all its manifestations” an evil to be ended, backed self-determination in named North African cases, urged disarmament and limits on nuclear weapons, and concluded with ten principles centered on sovereignty, equality, non-interference, peaceful settlement, cooperation, justice, and international obligations. These are observed documentary facts: they appear in the communiqué dated 24 April 1955. [1]

The delegates did not speak as one bloc on every issue. The communiqué itself acknowledged a mix of political systems and interests through its emphasis on sovereignty and voluntary cooperation; it explicitly said that economic consultation was not intended to form a regional bloc. In the immediate term, Bandung produced a shared text and a recommendation that the five sponsoring countries consider another meeting. It did not solve the conflicts discussed around it, nor did it create a single institution that could speak permanently for every Asian and African society. [1]

Named newspapers reveal how framing shaped the event. Tillman Durdin’s contemporaneous *New York Times* report of 18 April foregrounded the opening’s scale, Sukarno’s appeal for peace, and a proposed agenda designed to avoid sharp controversy over Israel and Formosa. A 25 April *Times* editorial, “After Bandung,” judged the conference an experiment that had brought diverse groups into free exchange, but also treated Zhou Enlai’s diplomacy principally through U.S. anxieties about the Taiwan Strait, Korea, Vietnam, and Communist “propaganda.” [2] [3] Cameron Duodu’s later *Guardian* opinion essay, published in 2007, instead described Bandung as an important gathering of peoples colonized or dominated by European powers or the United States, then criticized the limited practical cooperation that followed. [4]

That difference must remain visible in an interactive interpretation. The *Times* pieces are contemporaneous observation and editorial argument, not a neutral view from outside Cold War concerns. Duodu’s *Guardian* essay is a later assessment of Bandung’s legacy, not evidence of what delegates thought in the hall. Likewise, the often-invoked “spirit of Bandung” is a later interpretive shorthand. The U.S. Office of the Historian says the conference and its resolution laid a foundation for the later Non-Aligned Movement, while also stressing that participants held varied ideologies and loyalties. [5]

A respectful world should therefore end not with a triumphal claim that Bandung resolved colonialism, racism, or war, but with the final document on a table and its unfinished questions. The conference gave governments from Asia and Africa a consequential forum and a language of cooperation. Its promises—and the differences among those asked to carry them forward—belong in the story together. [1] [5]

## Reactor 3D world-generation prompt

**Create: “Bandung, 24 April 1955 — A Communiqué, Not a Conclusion.”** Render a 1:1, walkable historical-learning environment set on **Sunday, 24 April 1955, from 12:00–15:00 Western Indonesian Time**, inside and immediately outside **Merdeka Building (formerly Societeit Concordia), at the Asia Afrika–Braga corner in Bandung, Indonesia**. Treat the clock window as a design reconstruction, not a claim that the communiqué was signed at a documented minute. Build the 1940 international-style/Art Deco façade with horizontal rooflines, plaster walls and the nearby Cikapundung-side greenery; include the broad Asia Afrika street, period-appropriate paving, restrained 1950s vehicles, and a visual sightline toward the nearby Savoy Homann district. The building has just been renamed Merdeka. Use humid, mild Bandung highland daylight with broken cloud and soft shadows; label this weather as climate-consistent atmosphere rather than a documented forecast.

Use a human eye-level camera, 1.65 m high, a 28–35 mm equivalent lens, gentle walking speed, accessibility teleport points, and a physically plausible 1:1 scale. Exterior crowd level is calm and moderately busy: small groups of arriving and departing delegates, local residents, press workers, police/security at a respectful distance, and venue staff. Do not create a rally, riot, triumphal parade, or an anonymous “exotic” mass. Inside, preserve a formal committee-room scale: a limited number of seated delegates, interpreters only where source-confirmed language needs are explained, paper folders, name placards, period microphones, maps, typewritten drafts, and non-dramatic press equipment. Clothing is varied but specific: Indonesian batik shirts, kebaya, sarong and peci among local participants; mid-century suits, national dress, and modest formal clothing among international delegates. Avoid assigning costume to nationality by stereotype; no invented uniforms, modern logos, smartphones, flags not documented for participant states, or contemporary furniture.

Make five optional, source-labeled hotspots. **(1) “The hall changes meaning”** connects the former European social club to the renamed Merdeka Building, with a short sourced architectural timeline. **(2) “29 governments, many positions”** shows the sponsoring five and 24 additional participants on an interactive map; it must emphasize that governments did not represent every person or political view in Asia and Africa. **(3) “Drafting cooperation”** lets visitors inspect a facsimile-style, accessible excerpt of the Final Communiqué on technical cooperation, self-determination, anti-discrimination, disarmament, and the ten principles. **(4) “Read the press critically”** places the 18 and 25 April *New York Times* items beside Cameron Duodu’s 2007 *Guardian* essay, visibly labeling contemporary reporting, editorial opinion, and later retrospective interpretation. **(5) “Archive, not spectacle”** displays the three licensed Indonesian-government archival images with captions, source links, and rights metadata.

Narration should be calm, precise, and Indonesian-centered without being nationalist or triumphalist. State only documented facts as facts; use “historians later describe,” “the 2007 opinion argued,” or “this is an interpretive reconstruction” for interpretation. Address colonialism and racism in the language of the communiqué and with care for people affected; do not aestheticize oppression or turn geopolitical disagreement into entertainment. **Explicit exclusions:** no fabricated dialogue, no voice or face impersonation of Sukarno, Zhou Enlai, Nehru, Nasser, or any named person; no combat, weapon display, detention, disaster imagery, blood, protest confrontation, romanticized colonial décor, or AI-generated claims of private meetings. End on the unsigned-looking working drafts and the published final principles, with a prompt asking what cooperation can accomplish when participants retain serious differences.

## VEED-compatible avatar prompts

1. **Primary historian host — interpretive presenter.** A fictional Indonesian historian in her 40s, professionally dressed in a contemporary, understated batik-accented jacket, speaks directly to camera in a quiet museum-study setting. Use clear, measured English with Indonesian place names pronounced carefully. On-screen label: “Historian host — interpretive educational presentation, not a historical witness.” Explain sources, distinguish the 1955 record from later legacy claims, and never imitate a living person or a named historical leader.
2. **Historically situated witness/role — interpretive reconstruction.** A fictional composite of a Bandung-based Indonesian venue volunteer in their early 20s in April 1955, wearing modest period-appropriate local formal attire and carrying a paper programme outside Merdeka Building. On-screen label at all times: “Interpretive reconstruction — fictional composite, not a recorded individual.” In first person, describe only ordinary, plausible logistical observations (“I am helping guests find the entrance”) and redirect all political claims to source cards; no invented dialogue with delegates, no claim to have witnessed private decisions, and no resemblance to any real person.
3. **Contemporary guide — reflective close.** A fictional Indonesian museum educator in their 30s of any gender, in present-day professional clothing, standing beside a digital facsimile of the Final Communiqué. On-screen label: “Contemporary guide — interpretation informed by archival sources.” Invite visitors to compare source types and consider the unfinished commitments on cooperation, self-determination, and non-discrimination. Avoid impersonating living people, advocacy slogans, or claims that every present-day community shares one view of Bandung.

## Interaction-design items

1. **Layered participant map:** Select a sponsoring state or participant government to see its name in 1955, its role in the conference, and a reminder that government representation was not the same as representing all residents.
2. **Communiqué table:** Visitors assemble five documented themes—economic cooperation, cultural cooperation, human rights/self-determination, dependent peoples, and peace—then open the exact source excerpt rather than a simplified “win” badge.
3. **Press comparison desk:** Toggle between the *New York Times* opening report, the *Times* editorial, and the *Guardian* retrospective, with colored labels for reportage, editorial, and later opinion.
4. **Merdeka Building time-layer:** A non-gamified slider explains the building’s earlier Concordia use, its 1955 renovation/renaming, and its present museum role, avoiding a progress narrative that erases colonial inequality.
5. **Archive-photo viewer:** Zoom only into three rights-cleared Indonesian-government images; captions identify date, source, rights, and what cannot be inferred from a single photograph.
6. **Reflection ledger:** Visitors choose one principle from the Final Communiqué and compare its wording with a question about implementation, differences, and historical limits; answers are private, not scored.

## Principal caveat

The most important pedagogical constraint is to avoid converting **“the spirit of Bandung”** into a homogeneous or inevitable story. The 29 governments had differing interests, ideologies, and levels of independence, while many people living under colonial rule had no government represented. The environment must distinguish the documented 1955 communiqué from later claims about its legacy, especially non-alignment, and must identify all weather, crowd, and witness details not supported by a primary source as reconstruction.

## Research method and limits

This note is based on pages opened and read from the sources above on 12 September 2026. It deliberately separates: (1) primary text and archival records; (2) contemporary newspaper reporting/editorial argument; (3) later institutional historical synthesis; and (4) retrospective newspaper opinion. It does not assert unverified weather, dialogue, exact crowd numbers beyond attributed reporting, or private interpersonal scenes. All three image candidates were checked on their individual Wikimedia Commons file pages; their stated license information, not any assumption about newspaper rights, determines their inclusion.
