# HMT *Empire Windrush* at Tilbury Docks — 22 June 1948

## Scope and editorial position

This note supplies historically grounded material for an interactive learning world about the arrival of HMT *Empire Windrush* at Tilbury Docks, Essex, on 22 June 1948. It treats the arrival as a particular event within longer histories of Caribbean mobility, British colonial rule, wartime service, post-war recovery, and racism. It avoids presenting the ship as the literal beginning of Black British history or Caribbean migration to Britain. The *SS Ormonde* arrived in Liverpool in March 1947 and the *Almanzora* arrived at Southampton in December 1947.[1] [2]

## Verified narrative spine

At early morning Tilbury, people stood on the deck of the *Empire Windrush* looking toward England after an Atlantic voyage. The newspaper account reprinted by *The Guardian* was filed from Tilbury on 23 June 1948 and identifies 492 men from Jamaica and Trinidad in the scene it described.[5] This is not the same measure as the whole ship’s manifest. Royal Museums Greenwich and Goldsmiths, University of London identify **1,027 passengers and two stowaways** on the ship’s arrival, while more than 800 passengers gave a Caribbean country as their last country of residence.[2] [4]

The difference is historically important rather than a reason to choose a single convenient number. A 1948 Assistance Board memorandum, held by The National Archives, records that the boarding party dealt with **492 passengers and 20 stowaways**. It places those people in three administrative groups: 52 Armed Forces volunteers, 236 accommodated in the Clapham deep shelter, and 204 dispersed from Tilbury to places they already had to go.[7] That memorandum describes an arrival-management cohort, whereas the passenger manifest captures the ship’s passengers. The learning world should label both counts and explain their distinct documentary contexts.

The reasons for travel varied. The National Archives records post-war work shortages in Britain, Caribbean wartime service, unemployment in Jamaica, and the legal position of Citizens of the United Kingdom and Colonies under the 1948 British Nationality Act.[1] The *Windrush* route included Trinidad, Kingston, Tampico, Havana, and Bermuda before Tilbury.[1] A pre-arrival telegram recorded 350 people without accommodation arrangements and named occupational aspirations including engineering, building, factory work, farming, mining, and re-enlistment in the RAF or Army.[6]

On arrival, accommodation and employment were immediately consequential. The Ministry of Labour set up a small labour exchange at the Clapham South deep shelter; the National Archives identifies jobs that followed in ironworks, railway works, tinplate works, catering, and elsewhere.[1] These facts should not be presented as evidence of an uncomplicated welcome. The National Archives and Imperial War Museums document racism and discrimination in work and housing after arrival.[1] [3] Two days after docking, 11 Labour MPs called for a halt to the “influx of coloured people,” according to Royal Museums Greenwich’s account of the related archival correspondence.[2]

## Full article — At Tilbury, an arrival became a lasting symbol

The quay at Tilbury was cool and grey when HMT *Empire Windrush* came in on the morning of 22 June 1948. From the ship’s rail, passengers could see the Essex dockside after a long crossing. A *Guardian* special correspondent, writing from Tilbury the next day, observed people looking down from the ship and described an official reception without a band.[5] That is a contemporary observation, not a complete account of what every arrival felt or experienced.

The voyage had brought together people with different histories and plans. The passenger manifest records 1,027 passengers and two stowaways; more than 800 passengers gave a Caribbean country as their last country of residence.[2] [4] It included men, women, and children, as well as people intending to settle and people whose journeys had other destinations. The *Guardian* report focused on 492 men from Jamaica and Trinidad, a narrower group within the scene it covered.[5] Its list of reported trades—builders, carpenters, mechanics, tailors, a musician, and a law student among them—helps counter the later stereotype of a uniform crowd.

The lead-up cannot be reduced to a single invitation to “rebuild Britain.” Britain had post-war labour shortages, and the 1948 British Nationality Act gave Citizens of the United Kingdom and Colonies the right to live and work in Britain.[1] Many Caribbean people had served in British forces during the Second World War; some sought to re-enlist or find civilian work. Conditions in the Caribbean also mattered: The National Archives identifies unemployment in Jamaica and damage from a recent hurricane.[1] Passage was advertised, cost £28 on the troop deck, and required people to make their own decisions and arrangements.[1] [5] The ship travelled via Trinidad, Kingston, Tampico, Havana, and Bermuda before Tilbury.[1]

Even the numbers at the dock tell a more careful story. A 1948 Assistance Board memorandum says the boarding party dealt with 492 passengers and 20 stowaways. It divided those people into 52 Armed Forces volunteers, 236 people accommodated in the Clapham deep shelter, and 204 dispersed directly from Tilbury to places they already had to go.[7] This administrative count is not a contradiction of the 1,027-person manifest. It documents a particular arrival-management group. A pre-arrival telegram had warned that 350 people had no accommodation arrangements and listed employment preferences including engineering, building, factory work, farming, mining, and military service.[6]

The immediate consequence was therefore not one shared destination. Some people continued to contacts or homes; others entered temporary shelter. At Clapham, the Ministry of Labour opened a small labour exchange, and The National Archives records placements in ironworks, railway works, tinplate works, and catering.[1] These arrangements mattered, but they did not guarantee equal treatment. Archive and museum accounts document discrimination in jobs and housing after arrival.[1] [3] Two days after the ship docked, 11 Labour MPs wrote to Clement Attlee seeking a halt to the “influx of coloured people,” an archival episode that complicates any simple description of an official welcome.[2]

The two newspapers illuminate different layers of the event. The *Guardian*’s 1948 report—republished in 2016 as an archive extract—uses the language and limits of its time. It focuses on the dockside, employment expectations, and a selective group of male arrivals; its sympathetic moments sit beside colonial assumptions that should be contextualised, not reproduced as neutral narration.[5] The *Independent*’s 2023 Windrush Day article is plainly retrospective. It connects the 1948 arrival with national commemoration and with later harms suffered when legally resident people faced documentation problems, loss of services, and threatened removal under the hostile-environment policy.[8] The later article cannot tell us what an individual at Tilbury saw in 1948, but it explains why the word “Windrush” carries public meaning beyond that morning.

Later interpretation has made this one arrival emblematic of the “Windrush generation,” usually understood as Commonwealth citizens who came to Britain from the Caribbean between 1948 and 1971.[2] That symbolic status is meaningful, but it should not erase earlier arrivals: the *SS Ormonde* docked in Liverpool in March 1947 and the *Almanzora* reached Southampton that December.[1] Nor should it turn passengers into symbols without agency. A respectful reconstruction should remain with the dockside facts—luggage, forms, uncertain destinations, skilled people, and a changing country—then invite learners to trace opportunities and discrimination through evidence rather than invented personal stories.

## Press context and handling

The 1948 *Guardian* special-correspondent report provides a contemporaneous media frame: it notices the absence of a band, records official welcome, lists varied trades, and foregrounds expectations about work. It is valuable as observation, but its language includes colonial assumptions and should never supply unmarked character narration.[5] Its coverage focuses on 492 men from Jamaica and Trinidad rather than the full range of the passenger list.

The *Independent*’s 22 June 2023 article is explicitly retrospective. It uses the 75th anniversary to connect the 1948 arrival to Windrush Day and to later harms caused by documentation failures and the Home Office’s hostile-environment policy.[8] It is not evidence for what a person at Tilbury saw in 1948. The two papers therefore serve different interpretive purposes: *The Guardian* offers contemporary framing of the dockside scene, while *The Independent* makes the event part of a later public reckoning. The world should clearly identify these layers.

## Reactor world-generation brief

Create a respectful, research-led 3D learning environment titled **“Tilbury Docks, 22 June 1948: Arrival and Next Steps.”** Lock the playable time window to **approximately 07:00–10:30 BST on Tuesday 22 June 1948**. Place the scene at **Tilbury Docks on the north bank of the River Thames, Essex**, looking across the river toward Kent; do not substitute a generic London dock, a modern terminal, or a tropical port. Begin at pedestrian eye height, 1.65m, on the quayside at 35mm-equivalent field of view. Support a slow, accessible walking pace; retain ship, quay, terminal, railings, gangway and distant industrial waterfront at credible human scale. Use a restrained long-lens establishing view only in the opening; then return to eye-level observation. The river is tidal and grey-brown. Use a cool, overcast early-summer morning with broken cloud, pale diffuse light, a light breeze, damp-looking working surfaces but no dramatic storm, and the modest visual residue of post-war Britain.

Model HMT *Empire Windrush* as a large converted passenger/troop ship moored beside a working quay, using verified vessel imagery only as a profile reference. Include dockside sheds, bollards, rope, cargo equipment, a modest reception/officials area, paper forms, clipboards, signs appropriate to 1948, luggage, trunks and suitcases. No anachronistic container stacks, security gates, smartphones, modern vehicles, post-1948 signage, NHS branding, or invented banners. The dock is not a festival and not a disaster scene.

Populate a **moderate, dispersed crowd**, never a homogenous mass: passengers of different ages and genders, including Caribbean people, other passengers, and British dock/official staff. Clothing should be plausible 1948 travel and work dress: suits, coats, hats, dresses, cardigans, sensible shoes, workwear and uniforms; a few sharply tailored suits are evidence-supported, but avoid caricature, costume excess, and a single visual stereotype. Some passengers wait, observe the shore, carry belongings, consult forms, or reconnect with known contacts. Officials organize categories quietly. Do not assign names, private thoughts, legal status, occupations, or family relations to generic characters. Do not clone identifiable historic people or synthesize their faces.

Create five optional, non-linear hotspots: (1) **Ship rail**—a map with route stops at Trinidad, Kingston, Tampico, Havana, Bermuda and Tilbury; (2) **arrival board**—a dual-count explainer: 1,027 passengers plus two stowaways on the manifest versus the Assistance Board’s 492-person arrival-management cohort; (3) **telegram desk**—a readable, transcribed 6 June telegram about accommodation and jobs, with language notes; (4) **gangway/officials area**—a decision-tree showing 52 Armed Forces volunteers, 236 people accommodated at Clapham deep shelter and 204 going directly to existing destinations; (5) **suitcase and labour-exchange map**—post-war labour needs alongside agency, paid passage and varied motives. A sixth quiet reflection point can compare contemporary Guardian reporting with retrospective Independent reporting and ask what each newspaper can and cannot tell us.

Narration is calm, precise, and invitational, voiced by a clearly labelled contemporary historian. Separate “A 1948 document records…” from “Later historians and commemorators interpret…” Never invent dialogue, internal monologue, sound bites, or conflict incidents. Ambient sound is low: water, gulls, engines at idle, subdued footfall, distant dock work; optional non-diegetic music is off by default. Use content notes before material on racism or later Home Office harms. Center people’s agency, occupations and choices without turning mobility or hardship into spectacle. Explicit exclusions: no slurs, racist taunts, violence, policing drama, detention imagery, poverty porn, celebratory “welcome to Britain” fantasy, “first-ever” claims, heroic-saviour framing, automatic use of steel pan or calypso as ambience, or an ending that implies discrimination was resolved. Cite sources in a pause menu and label all composite visuals as interpretive reconstruction.

## Interaction design

- **Two-document count comparator:** Learners toggle between the 1,027-passenger manifest and the Assistance Board’s 492-person arrival cohort, with the distinction explained before any quiz.
- **Route and decision map:** An interactive Atlantic route links port stops to individual agency, paid fare, post-war labour demand and colonial citizenship rather than a one-cause migration story.
- **Evidence tags:** Every hotspot carries “primary document,” “contemporary newspaper,” or “later interpretation,” and a short provenance statement.
- **Arrival pathways:** A calm, choice-based map shows the three recorded administrative pathways: Armed Forces volunteers, Clapham shelter, and direct dispersal. It does not frame any pathway as a game success or failure.
- **Press comparison panel:** A side-by-side, contextualized comparison shows the 1948 *Guardian* account and the 2023 *Independent* retrospective; learners identify their publication dates, scope and limits.
- **Quiet reflection exit:** Before leaving, learners choose a source-supported statement about continuity, contribution, discrimination, or historical uncertainty; there is no leaderboard or score for personal hardship.

## Principal caveat

The essential pedagogical constraint is **not to collapse a heterogeneous shipboard population into a single “Windrush passenger” story**. Sources use different denominators and frames: the contemporary Guardian’s 492 men from Jamaica and Trinidad, the Assistance Board’s 492-person operational cohort, and the passenger manifest’s 1,027 passengers plus two stowaways. Pair these documents, name the difference, and avoid attributing a generic experience, motive, welcome, occupation, or later outcome to every person on board.
